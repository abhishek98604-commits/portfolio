Talk track · 45 sec

Verizon sells two kinds of home internet. Fios is fiber, a line into the home. 5G Home is wireless, a signal from a tower to a box inside. Under 5G there are more levels, like LTE Home, and products like WiFi Backup that run on 5G.

Every address qualifies for only some of these. Loop qualification decides that.

Why this matters for the page: Fios shows an exact speed, 5G shows a range, and setup is different. So one plan tile had to handle all these variations.

Talk track · 1 min

The brief was simple: fix the drop on the plan selection page. Let me show it with rounded numbers for a typical month.

About 1 million visits check their address. About half, 500 thousand, can get service and reach the plan page.

That is where it breaks. Only 300 thousand pick a plan and move on. 200 thousand, 40 percent, leave right here.

In the end about 115 thousand order, which adds up to roughly 350 thousand new customers a quarter. So the final conversion is about 11 and a half percent of address checks.

The key point: we lose about 200 thousand shoppers a month on this one page. That is almost twice the number of people who buy. If we win back even 10 percent of them, that is about 20 thousand more customers a month.

The drop happens on mobile and desktop, and it is higher on 5G Home than on Fios. The goal was better overall conversion and more net adds, and since it is a monthly subscription, every shopper we keep is revenue every month.

From Verizon customer research

Talk track · 1 min

Instead of a long list of personas, it helped to think of two kinds of homes.

Young and urban: mostly 18 to 34, living in cities, and more often on 5G Home. They are value conscious. Comfortable online, often working from home, and happy to pay more if they see the value. Their question is, will gaming work fine?

Mature families: millennials to boomers, living in the suburbs, more often on Fios. They are price conscious, because one bill covers the whole family, so every dollar counts. And they are less familiar with tech. Their question is, will Netflix work for all of us?

Both care about two things: price, and the idea of speed. Not Mbps, but what it lets them do. So the tile had to explain price and speed in plain terms, and could not assume people know how things work.

Talk track · 1 min

Let me show you the current experience, so it is clear what we are talking about.

A shopper enters their address and we check what they can get. Then they land on plan selection. This is where about 40 percent of people leave.

Those who stay go through gifts, then perks, which are streaming add-ons like Netflix at a discount. Then Live TV, which is TV channels streamed through YouTube TV, and Home Phone, a landline over the internet connection. Then the cart and checkout.

Our focus is this one page, plan selection, the first real decision in the journey. TV and Home Phone have their own flows too, but this case study focuses on Internet.
