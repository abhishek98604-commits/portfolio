Talk track · 45 sec

Verizon sells two kinds of home internet. Fios is fiber, a line into the home. 5G Home is wireless, a signal from a tower to a box inside. Under 5G there are more levels, like LTE Home, and products like WiFi Backup that run on 5G.

Every address qualifies for only some of these. Loop qualification decides that.

Why this matters for the page: Fios shows an exact speed, 5G shows a range, and setup is different. So one plan tile had to handle all these variations.

Talk track · 1 min

Instead of a long list of personas, it helped to think of two kinds of homes.

Young and urban: mostly 18 to 34, living in cities, and more often on 5G Home. They are value conscious. Comfortable online, often working from home, and happy to pay more if they see the value. Their question is, will gaming work fine?

Mature families: millennials to boomers, living in the suburbs, more often on Fios. They are price conscious, because one bill covers the whole family, so every dollar counts. And they are less familiar with tech. Their question is, will Netflix work for all of us?

Both care about two things: price, and the idea of speed. Not Mbps, but what it lets them do. So the tile had to explain price and speed in plain terms, and could not assume people know how things work.

What we saw, screen by screen

Talk track · 1.5 min

Next, journey mapping. We printed the whole journey and mapped it in person, with leadership in the room. It gave us the wider picture.

We saw a lot of repetition, dead ends, and optional steps that looked required. The reason was simple: different parts of the journey were owned by different teams, and no one owned the whole journey. We highlighted this to leadership.

One insight stood out: less than 1 percent of orders added TV or Phone, yet they got the same space as internet.

Looking at the screens side by side, a few things jumped out. The gift offer showed up three times: on the plan card, as its own required step, and as a banner later. There were four different ways to choose things: radio buttons, toggles, Select buttons and plus rows. The same sign in banner repeated on every screen, pushing the plans down. And "optional" was small grey text, so people felt they had to choose perks.

All of this pointed to the same fixes: one way to choose, each thing shown once, and a clear, short path to checkout.

Next, a lot of good research already existed: more than 100 decks from the research team, plus PDFs, screen recordings and competitor studies.

Key highlight 2

Stack all plans, to make comparing easy.

First, make comparing easy. No single plan fits everyone. We tried that, and it did not work.

The old design made comparing hard. You had to swipe sideways, one plan at a time, with too much on each card. Try it on the left. So we stacked all plans one below the other, with less information. People see every plan on one screen and open the one that matters.

Talk track · 45 sec

Third, anything other than internet became optional. It was never mandatory, but the old design made it look that way.

Now, when you add a plan to cart, you land in a hub. Perks, Live TV and Home Phone wait there, and checkout is always one click away.

This was a trade-off, and my call. Fewer people may add TV or Phone. But they are only about 1% of the business, and a clear path for the 99% who want internet matters more.

Talk track · 1 min

Fourth, clear communication. We worked with the content team, reviewed every piece of text on the page, and agreed the changes with legal.

It was not just rewriting. We also removed things that did not help. For example, "Good news, Verizon Home Internet is available" sounded nice, but it told people nothing about what to do. We replaced it with a clear header, "Select your plan", and when internet can arrive. The promo banner and the Fios sign in box became one row: mobile customer, sign in.

We rewrote things too. Two prices became one. The Auto Pay toggle now says the saving, 10 dollars a month. The long gift paragraph became "2 special gifts on us".

And we moved things to where people decide. The 99 dollar setup note moved from the top into each plan, in plain words.

The hard part: we wanted labels like "great for 4 people". We did not have the data to prove it, and legal would not allow it without proof. So we kept Mbps for now. That is the first thing in version 2.

    Talk track · 1.5 min

    Where it stands. We tested the designs in a moderated study with real users, with the research team. Shoppers found it easier to compare plans, and they kept their context.

    We delivered more than 40 screens for mobile and desktop, about 80 use and edge cases, and one flexible card for Internet, TV and Phone. Along the way we added about 20 new mobile web patterns with the VDS team. It is now releasing in phases, measured on shoppers who move forward from the plan page.

    A few key learnings. First, designing for something people cannot touch or see, like internet. You cannot show it, so speed and value have to be explained in plain words.

    Second, shape the end-to-end journey before going deep into screens. That is how we arrived at hub and spoke.

    Third, AI sped up iteration, but the final quality still came down to design judgment and craft.

    And fourth, let stakeholders take part. Mapping the journey with leadership in the room, and ranking the card with the product lead, made decisions faster and easier to agree on.

    Before I end, one more thing. How will people buy home internet in the future?

    I believe it becomes a conversation, with all the information at your fingertips. It starts by asking what you need and your budget. It detects your location, but confirms it with you first. It recommends a plan with real reviews, and can play a short explainer video for your home. You can share the plan details with your family. It shows the offers and perks that come with the plan, compares live with other providers, and finds the discounts you qualify for. If the credit check fails, it gives you the best alternative.     How it works, simply: one shopping agent talks to you. It uses skills, things it knows how to do, like recommending, comparing or finding discounts. And it always checks Verizon's real data for plans, prices and offers, so it never makes up a price. And you confirm before anything happens.
